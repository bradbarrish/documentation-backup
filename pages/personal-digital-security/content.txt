By using it and attempting to keep up with all the advancements (impossible!), I’ve become much more aware of its uses, for wonderful things and awful things. Because of that, I feel particularly compelled to share some advice to help ensure your most sensitive digital information is protected and less likely to fall into the hands of criminals. Given the rapid pace of AI and some recent vulnerability disclosures, I’ve felt a sense of urgency to get people to take their digital security more seriously. Don’t wait to be hacked or compromised in some way before following this advice. You will spend way more time and money recovering from a hack.

Highly sophisticated hacking is now carried out, not just by experienced cyber-criminals, but by aspiring amateur cyber-criminals as well. The kind of hacks that professional cyber-criminals and nation states have been known to execute are now being performed by small groups and individuals armed with advanced AI tools and agents. AI is also changing the pace of vulnerability disclosures. This includes what are referred to as zero-day vulnerabilities. These are flaws unknown to the software or hardware company, so no fix exists yet.

Many of the institutions, infrastructure and systems that we take for granted are likely to be hacked in the near future. The same is true for individuals. And I’m not just referring to run-of-the-mill data breach notices you receive in the mail or the ransomware attacks you read about in the news, though they will continue happening with more frequency. These will be the kind of hacks that disrupt our lives and society in a way we haven’t experienced before. I’m not telling you any of this to intentionally scare you, but I do hope it raises enough concern that you are compelled to take actions to protect yourselves and those closest to you.

Here are a few things I recommend you do:


The easiest thing you can do, and the one that most people put off, is keeping your devices and apps up to date. Apply updates as soon as they are available, especially for operating systems like macOS and Windows and critically for web browsers. If you see a notification to update, do it! Don’t put it off.

Somewhat related, it’s a good idea to restart your computer and mobile device(s) at least once a month. For what it’s worth, I do this once a week. Restarting will help ensure security patches and other updates are installed, many of which cannot be completed until after a restart.

Use a password manager. I like 1Password and have used it for many years now. I have a family account and everyone in the family uses it. In my role as CTO, I’ve deployed it across the company. If you use Apple products, Apple Passwords is better than nothing, but I recommend that people not have their password manager tied to their Apple Account, just in case it’s compromised. For the same reasons, I also don’t recommend using Google’s built-in password manager.

Use only unique passwords that have been generated by a password manager for every site, service or login of any consequence. Chances are, your passwords have been compromised in a data breach, which is especially bad if you use the same or similar passwords in multiple places. And even if they haven’t leaked in a data breach, if you’re using simple combinations of words and numbers, you’re leaving your most sensitive information vulnerable. It’s just not worth the risk.

Use an authenticator app and enable two-factor authentication (2FA) on every account that has the option to do so. If you end up using 1Password, it’s probably easiest to use 1Password’s built-in authenticator. If you already use a different password manager, I recommend Ente Auth. If you already use an authenticator app, no need to switch! Start with the most important or sensitive accounts such as government and financial institutions, email, Apple, etc. Ideally you wouldn’t use your phone number/text messages as a second factor, but sometimes using an authenticator app isn’t an option.


A couple other things to keep in mind:


Assume any name or number calling or texting you is someone trying to scam or hack you. Caller ID can be spoofed and scammers use this all the time to their advantage. If someone says they’re calling from your bank, credit card company, Google, Apple, Microsoft or whatever, don’t talk to them or engage them in any way. Just hang up. If you’re especially concerned, call the institution or company on the number listed on their website.

Don’t open email attachments you aren’t expecting, even if they are from people you know or look totally legitimate. If you aren’t expecting it, don’t open it. The same is especially true for links.

Agentic AI tools like Meta Muse, ChatGPT dots, Instinct, Grok Bot and others are becoming increasingly popular. They can be useful, but only if you give them access to sensitive information like your email account, calendar or financial accounts. Unless you know exactly what you’re doing, or what the app is doing with your data, I’d advise against signing up for or installing them on your devices.
