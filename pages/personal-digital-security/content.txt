Maybe one of them shared a link to this document with you, which is totally fine! I wrote this document in October of 2026 and will do my best to keep it updated. I’ve written it entirely on my own and it was lightly edited by AI, but the words are mine.

I should start off by saying that I am not anti-AI, though I remain skeptical it will do everything the frontier AI companies claim it will do. This includes but is not limited to creating a mass extinction event, developing feelings or having taste. Regardless of how you personally feel about AI, it’s not going away. I firmly believe the only way to form your own opinions or discuss it intelligently is to actively use it.

I use AI every day, both in my personal and professional life. I haven’t been this excited about technology since the dawn of the web in the 1990s—maybe even since I started using computers as a kid. I’ve been actively experimenting with AI for the last four years and using it in increasingly sophisticated and advanced ways for the last couple of years. I code with it, I have it help me with personal things like tax prep, submitting insurance claims and I’ve even created a personal AI assistant that runs on its own dedicated computer at home. As a Chief Technology Officer, I’ve deployed AI across the company I work for and spent hundreds of hours upskilling myself and co-workers. While it’s incredibly useful and mind-blowing, it’s absolutely terrifying at the same time.

By using it and attempting to keep up with all the advancements, I’ve become much more aware of it’s uses, for wonderful things and awful things. Because of that, I feel particularly compelled to share some advice to help ensure people’s most sensitive digital information is protected and less likley to fall into the hands of criminals. Given the rapid pace of artificial intelligence and some recent vulnerability disclosures, this has become increasingly urgent. In my experience very few people are taking their digital security as seriously as they should and we’re at the point where getting hacked or compromised is a real possibility.

I’ve tried to make this advice as easy to follow as possible, but it still might be overwhelming for some. If that’s you, it’s absolutely worth finding someone technical who you can pay to help you. Please don’t wait to be hacked or compromised in some way before following this advice. You will spend way more time and money recovering from a hack.

Due to the absolutely insane pace and capabilities of AI, highly sophisticated hacking is now possible not just by experienced cyber-criminals, but by aspiring amateur cyber-criminals as well. In short, the kind of hacks that professional cyber-criminals and nation states have been known to execute are now being performed by small groups and individuals armed with advanced AI tools and agents. AI is also changing the pace of vulnerability disclosures. This includes what are referred to as zero-day vulnerabilities. These are previously unknown vulnerabilities in software and hardware. All of this combined with the recent and meteoric popularity of AI-powered digital assistants like Meta Muse, ChatGPT Dot, Instinct, Grok Bot and others is creating a perfect storm for some bad things to happen to individuals and businesses.

I’m not going to spend much time discussing these new agentic AI tools like Muse, but I would discourage most people from using them for now. If you want to try one of them out, I’d probably recommend ChatGPT dots over others and only if you have a ChatGPT Pro account with model traning turned off. In order for any of these apps to be useful—and they can be incredibly useful—they require you to grant them access to sensitive information. If you don’t know what you’re doing, or more importantly, what the app is doing with your data, don’t install these apps on your phone or computer.

Many of the institutions, infrastructure and systems that we have come to rely on in our lives are likely to be hacked in the near future. The same is true for individuals. And I don’t just mean run-of-the-mill data breaches that you get postcards for in the mail or ransomware attacks you read about in the news, though they will continue happening with more frequency. These will be the kind of hacks that disrupt our lives and society in a way we haven’t experienced before. I’m not telling you any of this to intentionally scare you, but I do hope it raises enough concern that you are compelled to take actions to protect yourself.

Here are a few things I recommend you do:


The easiest thing you can do, and the one that most people put off, is keeping your devices and apps up to date. Apply updates as soon as they are available, especially for operating systems like macOS and Windows and critically for web browsers. If you see a notification to update, do it! Don’t put it off.

Somewhat related, it’s a good idea to restart your computer and mobile device(s) at least once a month. For what its worth, I do this once a week. Restarting will help ensure security patches and other updates are installed, many of which cannot be completed until after a restart.

Use a password manager. I like 1Password and have been using it for many years now. I have a family account and everyone in the family uses it. In my role as Chief Technology Officer, I’ve deployed it across the company. If you are entirely in Apple’s ecosystem, Apple Passwords is better than nothing, but I recommend that people not have their password manager tied to their Apple ID in case their Apple ID is compromised. For the same reasons, I also don’t recommend using Google’s built-in password manager.

Use only unique passwords that have been generated by a password manager for every single site, service or login of any consequence. Chances are, your passwords have been compromised in a data breach. Even if they haven’t, if you’re using some simple combination of words and numbers, you’re leaving your most sensitive information vulnerable and it’s just not worth the risk. If you use the same password in more than one place, you’re especially vulnerable.

Use an authenticator app and enable two-factor authentication (2FA) on any account that has the option to do so. If you end up using 1Password, it’s probably easiest to use 1Password’s built-in authenticator. If you already use a different password manager, I recommend Ente Auth. Start with the most important or sensitive accounts such as government and financial institutions, email, Apple, etc. Ideally you would not use your phone number/text messages as a second factor, but sometimes using an authenticator app isn’t an option.


A couple other things to keep in mind:


Assume any name or number calling or texting you is someone trying to scam or hack you. Caller ID can be spoofed and scammers use this all the time to their advantage. If someone says they’re calling from your bank, credit card company, Google, Apple, Microsoft or whatever, don’t talk to them or engage them in any way. Just hang up. If you’re especially concerned, call the institution or company on the number listed on their website.

Don’t open email attachments you aren’t expecting, even if they are from people you know or look totally legitimate. If you aren’t expecting it, don’t open it. The same is especially true for links.
